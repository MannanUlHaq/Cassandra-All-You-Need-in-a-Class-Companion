## Physical Quantity

A physical quantity is a measurable property of an object or system that can be expressed as a numerical value combined with a unit. For example, mass, length, and temperature are all physical quantities because you can assign a number and a unit, like 65 kg or 300 K, to describe them. This concept is fundamental because it allows us to quantify and communicate observations about the physical world in a precise, universal way.

A physical property that can be measured and described by a number is called physical quantity.

This leads us to the definition of a 'Physical Quantity'. A physical quantity is simply any physical property that can be measured and described by a number. For instance, if you say the mass of a person is 65 kg, or the area of a hall is 100 square meters, you are describing physical quantities. A physical quantity is a measurable property of an object or system, expressed as a numerical value combined with a unit. For example, when you state a mass in kilograms or a length in meters, you are using numbers and units to quantify specific physical attributes. This concept is fundamental because it allows us to describe the physical world in a precise, consistent way that can be universally understood and used in calculations.

### Examples:

These are concrete instances that illustrate the abstract definition. They show how properties like mass, length, area, and temperature become measurable quantities when paired with a numerical value and a unit.

Mass of a person is 65 kg. Length of a table is 3 m. Area of a hall is 100 m$^2$. Temperature of a room is 300 K.

These are all examples of physical quantities. Each one describes a measurable property of an object or system using a specific numerical value and a standard unit of measurement. By giving the number and unit together, like 65 kg or 300 K, we can precisely communicate and compare these properties.

### Types of physical quantities

We classify physical quantities into two fundamental types: fundamental quantities and derived quantities.

#### Fundamental quantities:

Fundamental quantities are the basic building blocks of measurement in physics. They are independent, meaning we define their units without needing to refer to other quantities. The physical quantities which do not depend on any other physical quantities for their measurements are known as fundamental quantities.

**Examples:**
Mass, Length, Time, Temperature.

These are classic examples because you can measure each directly without referencing another physical quantity. They are the foundational building blocks for describing the physical world.

#### Derived quantities:

The second type is Derived Quantities. Derived quantities are measurements that are not fundamental themselves but are instead calculated by combining fundamental quantities. The physical quantities which depend on one or more fundamental quantities for their measurements are known as derived quantities.

As the name implies, these are quantities that depend on one or more fundamental quantities. For instance, speed is not a basic measurement; it is derived by dividing the fundamental quantity of distance by the fundamental quantity of time.

**Examples:**
Area, Volume, Speed, Force.

Area is a derived quantity because it is calculated by multiplying two lengths together. Volume is derived from length (e.g., length × width × height). Force is derived from mass, length, and time (as seen in Newton's second law: $F = ma$, where acceleration is length/time²).

### International System of units (SI)

To ensure that measurements are understood globally, we need a standard system. In 1971, the General Conference on Weights and Measures established the International System of Units, abbreviated as SI from its French name, *Système International d'Unités*. This system is the globally agreed-upon standard for measurement, created to ensure consistency in science and trade worldwide. The SI system consists of seven fundamental units and two supplementary units.

#### Rules for writing SI units

There are strict rules for writing SI units to ensure scientific communication is clear and consistent worldwide.

**Rule 1:** The full name of a unit always starts with a small letter, even if the unit is named after a person.

This rule ensures consistency by separating the unit itself from the person it honors. For example:
- The unit of force is written as **newton** (not Newton), named after Sir Isaac Newton.
- The unit of electric current is written as **ampere** (not Ampere), named after André-Marie Ampère.
- The unit of electric charge is written as **coulomb** (not Coulomb), named after Charles-Augustin de Coulomb.

This convention keeps unit names distinct from proper nouns and maintains a standard format across all measurements.

**Rule 2:** The symbol for a unit named after a scientist should be written as a capital letter. This rule honors the scientists whose work defined these fundamental units. For example, the unit of force is named after Isaac Newton, so its symbol is the capital 'N'. Similarly, the unit of electric current is named after André-Marie Ampère, giving us the symbol 'A'. Other examples include 'K' for kelvin (Lord Kelvin) and 'C' for coulomb (Charles-Augustin de Coulomb). Remember, this applies only to the unit symbol itself, not when you spell out the unit name in full (e.g., **newton**, **ampere**).

**Rule 3:** Symbols for all other units—those not named after scientists—are written in small letters. This rule helps quickly distinguish between units named after people and all other basic units. For instance, while you use a capital 'W' for watt (named after James Watt), you use a lowercase 'm' for meter, 's' for second, 'kg' for kilogram, and 'cd' for candela. This consistent system keeps scientific communication clear and universally understood.

**Rule 4:** One space must be left between the last digit of the numeral and the symbol of the unit. This rule ensures clarity and prevents misreading by visually separating the quantity from its unit. For example, the correct formatting is '10 kg', '5 N', and '15 m'. Writing them without the space, like '10kg', '5N', or '15m', is incorrect.

**Rule 5:** Unit symbols do not have plural forms. Even if the numerical value is greater than one, the symbol itself remains singular. Unit symbols are mathematical entities and are invariant. For example, you write '6 m' (for six metres), '14 kg', '20 s', and '18 K'. You should never write '6 ms', '14 kgs', '20 ss', or '18 Ks'. The spoken form may be plural ("six metres"), but the written symbol is always singular.

**Rule 6:** A full stop (period) should not be used after a unit symbol, unless the symbol occurs at the very end of a sentence. The unit symbol is not an abbreviation that requires a period. For example, it is correct to write "The mass is 25 kg" in the middle of a sentence. Writing "7 m.", "12 N.", or "25 kg." is incorrect unless the period is the one that ends the sentence itself.

**Rule 7:** No space is used between the symbols for units that form a compound unit. When writing a compound unit formed by multiplying two or more units, we do not add a space between their symbols. This rule ensures the unit is read as a single, coherent entity, distinguishing it from separate units listed in sequence. For example, the correct way to write the unit for energy, newton-meter, is "Nm" and not "N m". Similarly, joule-seconds is written as "Js", and volt-amperes as "VA". The space is reserved to separate the numerical value from the unit itself, so the correct formatting is `4 Js`, `19 Nm`, and `25 VA`. Incorrect examples would be `4 J s`, `19 N m.`, or `25 V A.`.

Each dimension is a unique category of measurement, and every physical quantity you encounter can be expressed as a combination of these basic building blocks. Using these symbols in square brackets provides a universal language for analyzing the relationships between different quantities.

The seven fundamental quantities are:

*   Mass

*   Current
*   Amount of substance

Length is [L], Mass is [M], Time is [T], Temperature is [K], and so on. These seven dimensions are the foundational building blocks for measuring everything in physics. Each dimension, like length [L] or mass [M], corresponds to a fundamental physical quantity that cannot be expressed in terms of the others. By using this standardized set of symbols, we can analyze and relate different physical quantities in a consistent way.

**Dimensions [L] [M] [T] [K] [A] [N] [J]**

These symbols represent the seven base dimensions of physical measurement, derived from fundamental quantities like length and mass. Using them in square brackets, such as [L] for length or [T] for time, is the standard way to denote the dimensional formula of any derived unit. This system allows us to check the consistency of equations and convert between different units of measurement.

### Dimensions of a Physical Quantity

So, what are the 'dimensions of a physical quantity'? The dimensions of a physical quantity tell you exactly how it is built from the fundamental base quantities like mass, length, and time. For example, speed has dimensions of length divided by time, which means it is derived from those two fundamental quantities raised to specific powers (length to the power of 1, time to the power of -1). So, stating the dimensions is a concise way of describing a quantity's fundamental composition, independent of the specific units used to measure it.

The powers of fundamental quantities in a derived quantity are called dimensions of that quantity. This refers to the powers to which the fundamental quantities must be raised in order to represent a derived quantity. Think of dimensions as the fundamental "recipe" for a physical quantity. For example, speed is length divided by time, so its dimensions are length to the power of 1 and time to the power of -1. These exponents tell you exactly how the basic building blocks combine to create the derived measurement.

Since Density is Mass divided by Volume, and Volume is length cubed, the dimensional formula becomes Mass divided by Length cubed. Dimensions tell us how a physical quantity is built from fundamental units like mass, length, and time. For density, you start with mass and divide by volume; since volume is length multiplied by itself three times, its dimension is length cubed. Therefore, the dimensional formula condenses to mass divided by length cubed, or M L⁻³.

**Example:**
$$
\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}
$$
$$
\begin{array}{c} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{length} \times \text{breath} \times \text{height}} \end{array}
$$
$$
\left[\text{Density}\right] = \frac{\left[\mathrm{M}\right]}{\left[\mathrm{L}\right] \times \left[\mathrm{L}\right] \times \left[\mathrm{L}\right]} = \frac{\left[\mathrm{M}\right]}{\left[\mathrm{L}^3\right]} = \left[\mathrm{M L^{-3}}\right]
$$
Hence the dimensions of density are 1 in mass and -3 in length.

### Uses of Dimension

Why do we bother with dimensions? There are three main uses: To check if a physics equation is mathematically correct, to convert between different units, and to derive new formulas entirely. Dimensions are a powerful tool in physics because they allow you to verify the logical consistency of an equation—both sides must have the same fundamental units. Beyond this check, you can use dimensional analysis to systematically convert measurements from one unit system to another and even to predict the form of new equations by considering what units the answer must have.

**To check the correctness of equation To convert units To derive a formula**

We bother with dimensions because they act as a built-in sanity check for any physics equation—if the units on both sides don't match, the equation is fundamentally flawed. Beyond that, dimensional analysis is a powerful tool for converting quantities between different measurement systems and can even guide us in piecing together the correct form of new equations when we understand the relevant physical variables.

#### To Check the Correctness of an Equation

First, let's look at checking the correctness of an equation. Checking an equation's correctness often starts with dimensional analysis. This means verifying that every term in the equation has the same fundamental dimensions, like length, mass, or time. If all terms share the same dimensions, as they do in the displacement equation, it's a strong sign the equation is physically plausible.

Consider the equation of displacement,
$$
\Delta x = v_i t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2
$$
By writing the dimensions we get,
$$
\Delta \mathbf{x} = \text{displacement} = [\mathrm{L}]
$$
$$
\mathrm{v_i t} = \text{velocity} \times \text{time} = \frac{\text{length}}{\text{time}} \times \text{time} = [\mathrm{L}]
$$
$$
\mathrm{at}^2 = \text{acceleration} \times \text{time}^2 = \frac{\text{length}}{\text{time}^2} \times \text{time}^2 = [\mathrm{L}]
$$

The dimensions of each term are same, hence the equation is dimensionally correct. If we analyze the dimensions, we see that every term—displacement, velocity times time, and acceleration times time squared—all simplify to the dimension of Length [L]. Since the dimensions match, the equation is correct. This equation is checked using dimensional analysis, which ensures all terms represent the same physical kind of quantity. Here, displacement, velocity multiplied by time, and acceleration multiplied by time squared all have the fundamental dimension of length. When every term in an equation shares the same dimension, the equation is at least dimensionally consistent, which is a necessary first step in verifying its correctness.

#### To Convert Units

Second, we can use dimensions to convert units. Suppose we want to convert Newtons (the SI unit of force) into Dynes (the CGS unit). We can use dimensional analysis to translate a quantity from one system of units to another. Since force has dimensions of mass, length, and time ([MLT⁻²]), we compare how each base unit differs between the systems.

Let us convert newton (SI unit of force) into dyne (CGS unit of force).
The dimensions of force are $[\mathrm{LMT}^{-2}]$.

So,
$$
1\,\text{newton} = (1\,\mathrm{m})(1\,\mathrm{kg})(1\,\mathrm{s})^{-2}
$$
and
$$
1\,\mathrm{dyne} = (1\,\mathrm{cm})(1\,\mathrm{g})(1\,\mathrm{s})^{-2}
$$

Thus,
$$
\frac{1\,\text{newton}}{1\,\text{dyne}} = \left( \frac{1\,\mathrm{m}}{1\,\mathrm{cm}} \right) \left( \frac{1\,\mathrm{kg}}{1\,\mathrm{g}} \right) \left( \frac{1\,\mathrm{s}}{1\,\mathrm{s}} \right)^{-2} = \left( \frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{cm}} \right) \left( \frac{1000\,\mathrm{g}}{1\,\mathrm{g}} \right) \left( \frac{1\,\mathrm{s}}{1\,\mathrm{s}} \right)^{-2}
$$
$$
= 100 \times 1000 = 10^5
$$

Therefore,
$$
1 \text{ newton} = 10^5 \text{ dyne}.
$$

#### To Derive a Formula

Finally, we can use dimensions to derive a formula. We can use dimensional analysis to find the correct relationship between physical quantities when we know which factors are involved. By equating the dimensions on both sides of a proposed equation, we solve for unknown exponents, leading us to the formula's structure.

The time period ‘T’ of oscillation of a simple pendulum depends on length ‘l’ and acceleration due to gravity ‘g’. Imagine we know the period of a pendulum 'T' depends on length 'l' and gravity 'g', but we don't know the exact equation. We start by assuming the time period T is proportional to powers of its influencing factors: length (l) and gravitational acceleration (g). By writing an equation with unknown exponents and then substituting the dimensions of each quantity, we balance the dimensions on both sides.

Let us assume that,
$$
\mathrm{T} \propto l^{\mathrm{a}}\ g^{\mathrm{b}} \qquad \mathrm{or} \qquad \mathrm{T} = \mathrm{K}\ l^{\mathrm{a}}\ g^{\mathrm{b}}
$$

Here, K is a constant which is dimensionless. In dimensional analysis, the constant K is a pure number that has no physical dimensions itself. It arises because our proportionality only matches the dimensions on both sides of the equation, not the exact numerical value.

Now, we write the dimensions of each quantity:
$$
\text{Dimensions of } \mathrm{T} = [\mathrm{L}^0 \mathrm{M}^0 \mathrm{T}^1]
$$
$$
\text{Dimensions of } l = [\mathrm{L}^1 \mathrm{M}^0 \mathrm{T}^0]
$$
$$
\text{Dimensions of } g = [\mathrm{L^1 M^0 T^{-2}}]
$$

Substituting these into our assumed equation:
$$
\left[\mathrm{L}^0\mathrm{M}^0\mathrm{T}^1\right] = \mathrm{K} \left[\mathrm{L}^1\mathrm{M}^0\mathrm{T}^0\right]^{\mathrm{a}} \left[\mathrm{L}^1\mathrm{M}^0\mathrm{T}^{-2}\right]^{\mathrm{b}}
$$
$$
= \mathrm{K} \left[ \mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{a}} \mathrm{M}^{\mathrm{0}} \mathrm{T}^{\mathrm{0}} \right] \left[ \mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{b}} \mathrm{M}^{\mathrm{0}} \mathrm{T}^{-2\mathrm{b}} \right]
$$
$$
\left[ \mathrm{L}^0 \mathrm{M}^0 \mathrm{T}^1 \right] = \mathrm{K} \left[ \mathrm{L}^{a+b} \mathrm{M}^0 \mathrm{T}^{-2b} \right]
$$

For this equation to be dimensionally consistent, the exponents for each fundamental dimension must be equal on both sides.

Comparing exponents:
For L: $a + b = 0$
For T: $-2b = 1$

Solving these equations:
$$
\mathrm{b} = -\frac{1}{2} \qquad \& \qquad \mathrm{a} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

Substituting the exponents back into the original equation:
$$
\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{K} \, l^{1/2} \, g^{-1/2}
$$
$$
\therefore \quad \mathrm{T} = \mathrm{K} \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}
$$

This method gives us the structure of the formula, showing that the period T is proportional to the square root of (l/g). The dimensionless constant K is determined by experiment; for a simple pendulum, it is found to be $2\pi$.
